Why Is Mars Red? The Science Behind the Red Planet

Mars has fascinated humans for centuries.

Even without a telescope, ancient observers could notice its distinctive reddish appearance in the night sky. Today, spacecraft have revealed a world covered by deserts, enormous volcanoes, deep valleys and polar ice caps.

But why is Mars red?

The answer is surprisingly simple: rust.

Mars Is Covered in Iron

The surface of Mars contains a large amount of iron.

When iron reacts with oxygen, it can form iron oxides—the same family of compounds responsible for the reddish-brown colour of rust on Earth.

Mars does not have oceans and an atmosphere like Earth's today, so its iron-rich surface has evolved differently. Fine dust containing iron minerals has spread across much of the planet.

That dust gives Mars its characteristic reddish-orange appearance.

How Did the Iron Become Oxidized?

Scientists are still studying exactly how Mars' iron became oxidized.

One possibility is that ancient Mars had more water and a thicker atmosphere than it does today. Water and atmospheric chemistry could have contributed to oxidation.

Another possibility involves tiny particles and atmospheric processes that gradually altered iron-bearing minerals over enormous periods.

Whatever the precise mechanism, the resulting iron oxides became mixed into Martian dust.

Strong winds then transported this dust across the planet.

Mars Is Not Completely Red

Although Mars is commonly called the Red Planet, its surface contains many different colours.

Spacecraft images show shades of brown, orange, yellow, grey and even darker tones.

The colour depends on the minerals present, the amount of dust covering a particular region and lighting conditions.

Some areas are relatively dark because exposed rocks contain less of the fine reddish dust that dominates other parts of the planet.

Giant Dust Storms Can Cover the Planet

Mars has a thin atmosphere, but it can still produce powerful dust storms.

Small dust clouds can grow and merge into enormous storms covering huge areas of the planet.

During particularly large events, dust can spread around much of Mars.

These storms can dramatically change the planet's appearance when viewed from space.

They can also create serious challenges for spacecraft operating on or near the Martian surface.

Mars Once Had Much More Water

Today, liquid water is not stable on the surface of Mars for long periods under most conditions.

But geological evidence indicates that ancient Mars was very different.

Scientists have found signs of ancient river channels, lake beds and minerals that typically form in the presence of water.

Billions of years ago, Mars may have had a thicker atmosphere and a warmer environment capable of supporting stable surface water.

That ancient water may have played a role in shaping the minerals that now cover the planet.

Mars Has Enormous Volcanoes

Mars is home to Olympus Mons, the largest known volcano in the Solar System.

It rises roughly 22 kilometres above the surrounding plains, making it nearly three times the height of Mount Everest above sea level.

Mars could develop such enormous volcanoes partly because its surface gravity is much weaker than Earth's.

The planet also lacks Earth's active plate tectonic system, allowing volcanic structures to remain in the same region and grow over extremely long periods.

The Planet Has Huge Canyons Too

Mars is not only a world of volcanoes.

It also contains Valles Marineris, a gigantic canyon system stretching thousands of kilometres across the surface.

It is far larger than the Grand Canyon.

The enormous valley system provides evidence of the powerful geological forces that have shaped Mars throughout its history.

Why Does Mars Have Such a Thin Atmosphere?

Mars' atmosphere is mostly carbon dioxide and is much thinner than Earth's.

Its lower gravity makes it easier for atmospheric gases to escape into space.

Mars also lacks the strong global magnetic protection Earth once had in its early history, allowing the solar wind to interact with the upper atmosphere.

Over immense periods of time, Mars lost much of the atmosphere it once possessed.

As the atmosphere became thinner, the planet became colder and less capable of maintaining liquid water on its surface.

Could Mars Have Once Supported Life?

This is one of the biggest questions surrounding the planet.

Mars is not known to contain life today, but ancient Mars may have provided environments that were more suitable for life.

The presence of ancient water is especially important because water is considered one of the key ingredients required by life as we understand it.

Scientists are searching for signs of ancient microbial activity in Martian rocks and sediments.

Future missions could provide even stronger evidence about whether Mars was ever inhabited.

Mars Is Still Changing

Mars may look like a dead, frozen world, but it is not completely inactive.

Dust moves across the surface.

Ice changes with the seasons.

The atmosphere expands and contracts as carbon dioxide freezes and evaporates near the poles.

Meteorites continue to strike the surface.

The planet is still evolving, just at a different pace from Earth.

The Red Planet Holds a Deep History

Mars is red because iron-rich materials on its surface have undergone oxidation, creating the dusty colours we see from Earth.

But that colour tells only part of the story.

Beneath the red dust is a planet with evidence of ancient water, enormous volcanoes, gigantic canyons and a dramatically changing atmosphere.

Mars may have once been warmer and wetter than it is today.

That possibility makes the Red Planet more than a beautiful object in the night sky.

It may be a geological record of how a potentially habitable world can transform into the cold desert we see today.
