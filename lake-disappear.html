Why Do Some Lakes Disappear Over Time?

Geography & Natural Science

A lake can look permanent.

It may have existed for hundreds or even thousands of years, surrounded by forests, villages or mountains. Yet lakes can shrink dramatically and sometimes disappear altogether.

This can happen naturally, through climate changes and geological processes, or because of human activity.

Lakes Are Not Permanent Containers

A lake exists because water is entering a basin faster than it is being lost.

Water can enter through:

  • Rivers
  • Rainfall
  • Snowmelt
  • Underground springs
  • Groundwater

Water leaves through evaporation, rivers, underground drainage and human extraction.

If the balance changes significantly, the lake begins to shrink.

Evaporation Can Remove Huge Amounts of Water

Hot and dry conditions can cause substantial evaporation.

When a lake loses water faster than it receives it, its level falls.

If dry conditions continue for years or decades, the change can become dramatic.

Shallow lakes are particularly vulnerable because a relatively small drop in water level can expose large areas of the lakebed.

Sediment Slowly Fills Lakes

Rivers do not carry only water.

They also transport mud, sand and other sediment.

When a river enters a lake, its water often slows down. The sediment it carries can then settle on the lakebed.

This process happens slowly, but over very long periods it can reduce the lake's depth.

Eventually, vegetation may begin growing in the newly exposed areas.

The lake gradually becomes a wetland and may eventually transform into dry land.

Plants Can Accelerate the Process

Plants require water, but they can also contribute to a lake's long-term transformation.

Aquatic plants grow in shallow areas and trap sediment.

As vegetation expands, more material accumulates.

Dead plants add organic matter to the bottom of the lake.

Over time, these processes can make parts of the lake shallower.

The lake begins changing from an open body of water into a more complex wetland environment.

Climate Change Can Alter Lake Levels

Changes in temperature and rainfall can significantly affect lakes.

Higher temperatures can increase evaporation.

Changes in rainfall can reduce the amount of water entering a lake.

Changes in snowpack can also affect lakes that depend heavily on seasonal snowmelt.

A lake does not need to experience a dramatic drought in a single year to decline.

Small changes repeated over many years can produce major effects.

Human Water Use Can Shrink Lakes

People depend heavily on freshwater.

Water may be removed from rivers or underground sources for agriculture, cities and industry.

If a lake relies on these water sources, excessive extraction can reduce the amount of water reaching it.

In some regions, irrigation has played a major role in reducing lake levels.

The problem becomes particularly serious when water consumption continues while natural water supplies decline.

Dams Can Change Lake Systems

Dams can dramatically alter the movement of water.

A dam may create a reservoir upstream while reducing water flow downstream.

This can change the natural balance of nearby lakes and wetlands.

Dams can also trap sediment that would otherwise move downstream.

That can affect ecosystems and landscapes far beyond the dam itself.

Some Lakes Disappear Suddenly

Not all lake disappearance is gradual.

Certain lakes can drain rapidly when natural barriers collapse.

A lake may be held back by loose sediment, volcanic material, ice or other geological structures.

If the barrier fails, water can escape through a newly created channel.

In extreme cases, a lake can lose much of its water surprisingly quickly.

Geological Changes Can Destroy Lakes

The land beneath and around a lake is not always stable.

Earthquakes, volcanic activity, landslides and gradual changes in the landscape can alter drainage patterns.

If water gains a new route out of a basin, the lake may shrink.

A geological event can therefore change the history of a lake almost instantly.

Disappearing Lakes Can Leave Strange Landscapes

When a lake disappears, it does not necessarily leave behind an empty hole.

It may leave salt flats, wetlands, grasslands, mudflats or exposed sediment.

In some places, ancient shorelines remain visible long after the water has disappeared.

These features provide clues about how large the lake once was.

Scientists can use them to reconstruct past climates and landscapes.

A Lake Can Also Return

Disappearing does not always mean permanent.

If rainfall increases, water use decreases or natural drainage changes, a shrinking lake can recover.

Some lakes fluctuate naturally between periods of expansion and contraction.

Their size can therefore change dramatically without the lake permanently disappearing.

Final Thoughts

Lakes may look like permanent features of the landscape, but they are constantly changing.

Water enters and leaves. Sediment accumulates. Plants spread. Climate shifts. Rivers change direction. Human activity alters water supplies.

Given enough time, these processes can transform a large lake into a wetland, a dry basin or something completely different.

A lake is not simply a body of water.

It is a temporary balance between water, land, climate and time.
