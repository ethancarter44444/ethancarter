Why Does the Moon Look Different Every Night?

Astronomy & Natural Science

The Moon appears to change shape throughout the month, sometimes looking like a thin crescent and sometimes appearing as a bright, complete circle. These changing appearances are called lunar phases. Although the Moon itself does not produce light, it reflects sunlight, and the amount of its illuminated surface visible from Earth changes as the Moon travels around our planet.

The Moon takes about 29.5 days to complete the full cycle of phases as seen from Earth. During this period, the Moon moves around Earth while Earth continues moving around the Sun. Because the Moon is constantly changing its position relative to Earth and the Sun, the angle from which we see its sunlit half also changes.

At any given moment, roughly half of the Moon is illuminated by the Sun. This fact does not change during the lunar cycle. What changes is how much of that illuminated half is facing Earth. When the Moon is positioned between Earth and the Sun, the side facing Earth receives very little direct sunlight, creating what is called a new moon. The Moon is still in the sky, but its illuminated side is mostly facing away from us.

As the Moon moves farther around Earth, a small portion of its illuminated side becomes visible. This produces the thin crescent shape often associated with the beginning of the lunar cycle. Each night, the visible illuminated portion generally grows larger, a process called waxing.

Eventually, the Moon reaches its first quarter phase. Despite the name, this does not mean that only one-quarter of the Moon is illuminated. About half of the Moon's visible disk appears illuminated from Earth. The term "first quarter" refers to the Moon being roughly one-quarter of the way through its orbit around Earth.

The Moon continues moving around Earth, and more of its sunlit side becomes visible. This produces the waxing gibbous phase, during which more than half of the visible disk is illuminated but the Moon has not yet reached full moon.

A full moon occurs when Earth is positioned approximately between the Sun and the Moon. The side of the Moon facing Earth is then almost completely illuminated by sunlight, making the Moon appear as a bright circular disk in the night sky.

After the full moon, the illuminated portion begins to decrease. This process is called waning. The Moon passes through the waning gibbous phase and eventually reaches the third quarter, when approximately half of its visible surface appears illuminated again.

The Moon then becomes a waning crescent. The illuminated section gets progressively smaller until the cycle reaches another new moon. The entire sequence then begins again.

The Moon's phases are sometimes misunderstood because people assume they are caused by Earth's shadow falling across the Moon. That is not what normally happens. Earth's shadow causes a lunar eclipse, which is a relatively uncommon event. Ordinary lunar phases occur because of the changing geometry between the Sun, Earth, and Moon.

The Moon's orbit is also slightly tilted relative to Earth's orbit around the Sun. This is one reason we do not experience a solar or lunar eclipse every month. During most new and full moons, the Moon passes slightly above or below the exact alignment required for an eclipse.

The changing phases have influenced human societies for thousands of years. Before modern calendars existed, people could use the Moon's repeating cycle to track the passage of time. Lunar cycles influenced calendars, cultural traditions, navigation, agriculture, and religious observances in many civilizations.

The Moon's appearance can also be affected by Earth's atmosphere. Near the horizon, the Moon may appear yellow, orange, or reddish because its light passes through a greater thickness of atmosphere before reaching our eyes. Dust, pollution, smoke, and other particles can further change its appearance.

The next time the Moon looks different from the previous night, nothing has happened to its shape. The Moon remains essentially spherical. What has changed is its position in space and the amount of its sunlit surface that happens to be visible from Earth.

The familiar sequence from new moon to crescent, quarter, gibbous, full moon, and back again is a simple consequence of orbital motion and sunlight. The Moon is not changing shape at all. We are simply seeing different portions of the same illuminated sphere as it travels around Earth.
