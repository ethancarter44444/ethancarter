Why Do We Get Brain Freeze? The Science Behind the Sudden Ice-Cream Headache

Few sensations are as strangely specific as brain freeze.

You take a large bite of ice cream or drink something extremely cold. A few seconds later, a sharp pain suddenly appears in your forehead. It can be intense, but thankfully it usually disappears almost as quickly as it arrives.

Despite its name, a brain freeze does not mean that your brain is actually freezing.

The real explanation involves the nerves and blood vessels around the roof of your mouth.

What Is Brain Freeze?

Brain freeze is commonly called an ice-cream headache.

Its scientific name is cold-stimulus headache.

The pain usually occurs after something very cold comes into contact with the roof of the mouth or the back of the throat.

The sensation can spread toward the forehead, temples, or area around the eyes.

Interestingly, the brain itself does not freeze.

The temperature of the brain remains far above the freezing point.

Why Does Cold Food Cause Head Pain?

The roof of your mouth contains numerous nerves and blood vessels.

When extremely cold food or liquid touches this area, it can cause a rapid change in temperature.

The body responds to that sudden cold stimulus.

Blood vessels in the region can constrict and then rapidly change their diameter as the area warms again.

These changes activate sensory nerves, particularly branches associated with the trigeminal nerve.

The trigeminal nerve is heavily involved in transmitting sensations from the face and head.

This is one reason a sensation occurring inside the mouth can be perceived as pain in the forehead.

Why Does the Pain Feel Like It Is Inside Your Head?

This is perhaps the strangest part.

The cold stimulus occurs in your mouth, but the pain seems to come from somewhere else.

This phenomenon is related to referred pain.

Different sensory signals can converge along related nerve pathways. As a result, the brain may interpret a signal originating near the roof of the mouth as pain coming from another area of the head.

The brain is essentially receiving information from a complex network and assigning the sensation to a location that does not exactly match the original stimulus.

Why Does It Happen So Quickly?

Brain freeze can appear within seconds.

That is because the trigger is not a slow change in the body's overall temperature.

Instead, a small area of tissue experiences a sudden cold stimulus.

The nerves in that area respond rapidly, producing a sensation that can develop almost immediately.

The speed is one reason brain freeze feels so surprising.

One moment you are enjoying a cold dessert.

The next moment, your forehead hurts.

Why Does Brain Freeze Usually Disappear Quickly?

The pain usually lasts only a short time.

Once the cold food or drink is removed, the temperature of the affected area begins returning toward normal.

The associated nerve and blood-vessel responses also settle down.

As the triggering stimulus disappears, the pain generally fades.

This is very different from many other types of headaches, which can persist for much longer periods.

Eating Too Quickly Can Make It Worse

People who consume very cold food rapidly may be more likely to experience brain freeze.

A large amount of cold material can cool the roof of the mouth quickly and intensely.

Eating slowly gives the mouth more time to warm between bites.

This is one reason people who are prone to brain freeze may notice that slowing down makes a difference.

Why Doesn't Everyone Get It?

Not everyone experiences brain freeze in the same way.

Some people rarely experience it, while others seem to get it almost every time they consume something extremely cold.

Individual differences in nerve sensitivity, anatomy, eating habits, and exposure to cold stimuli may contribute.

There is also evidence that people who experience certain types of headaches may be more susceptible to cold-stimulus headaches.

Can You Stop Brain Freeze?

The simplest approach is to stop consuming the cold food or drink.

You can also warm the roof of your mouth by pressing your tongue against it.

Taking a break allows the temperature of the affected area to return toward normal.

Drinking something at a more comfortable temperature can also help.

The key is removing the cold stimulus rather than trying to fight through it.

Why Is It Called "Brain Freeze"?

The name is more descriptive than scientific.

The sensation can feel as though something inside the head has suddenly become extremely cold.

But the brain itself is not freezing.

The term became popular because it captures the bizarre experience in two simple words.

Scientists generally use terms such as cold-stimulus headache instead.

A Small Pain With a Big Lesson

Brain freeze is a useful example of how complicated the body's sensory system can be.

The location where a stimulus occurs is not always the location where we perceive pain.

Our brains constantly interpret signals from networks of nerves and construct a picture of what is happening inside and outside the body.

Sometimes that interpretation produces surprising results.

A cold spoonful of ice cream touches the roof of your mouth, and your forehead complains.

Final Thoughts

Brain freeze may seem like a silly consequence of eating ice cream too quickly, but it reveals a fascinating feature of human biology.

The brain is not actually freezing. Instead, cold-sensitive nerves and blood vessels around the mouth trigger signals that the brain interprets as a brief headache.

So the next time you experience that familiar stabbing sensation, remember:

Your ice cream didn't freeze your brain. Your nervous system simply reacted very dramatically to the cold.
