Why Do Bridges Have Expansion Joints? The Hidden Science Keeping Roads Safe

Bridges may look rigid and immovable, but they are constantly expanding, contracting and flexing. Expansion joints give these enormous structures room to move safely.

When you drive across a bridge, the surface may look like one continuous piece of road.

But if you look closely, you may notice narrow gaps or metal strips crossing the roadway.

These are called expansion joints, and they perform an important job: allowing a bridge to move without damaging itself.

Bridges Move More Than You Think

A bridge may look completely rigid, but its materials constantly expand and contract.

Temperature is one of the biggest reasons.

When steel and concrete become hotter, they expand.

When they cool down, they contract.

The movement may be tiny, but on a long bridge, even a small change in material length can become significant.

Why Does Temperature Matter?

Imagine a steel bridge several hundred metres long.

During a hot summer afternoon, the entire structure can become significantly warmer than it was overnight.

The steel expands slightly.

When winter arrives, it contracts again.

The bridge therefore changes length repeatedly throughout the year.

Engineering Principle

Expansion joints provide controlled space for bridges to expand and contract as temperatures change.

What Would Happen Without Them?

If a long bridge were completely rigid and unable to expand or contract, temperature changes would create enormous internal stresses.

Those stresses could contribute to cracking, deformation or damage to structural components.

Instead of forcing the bridge to remain completely fixed, engineers allow controlled movement.

The bridge can expand and contract while the joints accommodate the change.

Bridges Also Move Under Traffic

Temperature isn't the only source of movement.

Large bridges can flex slightly when vehicles travel across them.

Heavy trucks, buses and other vehicles place changing loads on the structure.

Wind can also cause bridges to move.

Long suspension and cable-stayed bridges can experience noticeable movement as they respond to wind and traffic.

Expansion joints help accommodate some of these movements.

What Are Expansion Joints Made From?

Bridge expansion joints can use different designs and materials depending on the structure.

Some incorporate metal components.

Others use specialised rubber or flexible materials.

The joint must be strong enough to carry traffic while remaining flexible enough to accommodate movement.

This makes expansion joints a small but highly engineered part of a bridge.

Why Do Drivers Feel a Bump?

If you've ever driven across a bridge and felt a small thump-thump beneath your tires, you may have driven over an expansion joint.

The joint creates a controlled transition between sections of the bridge.

Ideally, it is smooth enough that drivers barely notice it.

However, expansion joints experience enormous amounts of traffic and environmental exposure, so they require regular inspection and maintenance.

Rain and Water Create Another Challenge

Expansion joints must also deal with water.

If water gets into areas where it shouldn't, it can contribute to corrosion and damage.

Engineers therefore design drainage systems and seals around many bridge joints.

Keeping water away from sensitive structural components can significantly extend the life of a bridge.

Why Maintenance Matters

Expansion joints experience repeated movement every day.

Thousands of vehicles can cross them, while temperatures continuously rise and fall.

Over time, seals can wear out, metal components can deteriorate and surfaces can become damaged.

A neglected expansion joint can eventually affect the surrounding roadway and structure.

That is why bridge inspections pay close attention to these seemingly small components.

A Small Gap Solves a Huge Engineering Problem

The next time you cross a bridge, look at the road beneath you.

That tiny gap is evidence of an important engineering principle.

Structures don't always need to be completely rigid to be strong.

Sometimes, the ability to move is what allows a structure to survive.

Expansion joints give bridges the freedom to expand, contract and flex while keeping the road safe and functional.

A small gap may not look impressive—but it can be essential to keeping hundreds of metres of infrastructure standing for decades.
